Book Reviews

The Dharma Bums

Jack Kerouac

★★★★★ Read December 29, 2025

“I wanted to...go off somewhere and find perfect solitude and look into the perfect emptiness of my mind and be completely neutral from any and all ideas."
“I saw that my life was a vast glowing empty page and I could do anything I wanted.”
"...I felt free and therefore I was free."

If you’ve only read On the Road, you’re missing out on Kerouac’s more mature, reflective work: The Dharma Bums. This is the Kerouac for readers drawn to nature writing, spiritual seeking, and the feeling of wanting less while wanting everything.

Where On the Road is velocity and appetite, The Dharma Bums slows down, and asks harder questions: how to live freely without becoming careless, how to seek enlightenment without abandoning joy, how to belong to the world without being owned by it.

Written in the late 1950s, at a moment when postwar America was accelerating toward conformity, consumerism, and Cold War anxiety, The Dharma Bums captures young people turning eastward, inward, and toward the natural world in search of meaning. Kerouac’s embrace of Zen Buddhism, environmental awareness, and voluntary simplicity would go on to shape the 1960s counterculture. In The Dharma Bums, this shift still feels intimate, provisional, and unresolved rather than ideological.

It belongs on the same shelf as books where the natural world is not a backdrop but a teacher, and where spiritual searching is inseparable from physical experience. There’s a clear throughline to Thoreau’s Walden, though Kerouac replaces solitude with friendship and transcendental calm with ecstatic, lived messiness.

Kerouac’s relationship to nature is expansive, overwhelming, and deeply emotional:

"The woods do that to you, they always look familiar, long lost, like the face of a long-dead relative, like a piece of forgotten song drifting across the water… most of all like golden eternities of past childhood or past manhood and all the living and the dying."

"Trails are like that: you're floating along in a Shakespearean Arden paradise and expect to see nymphs and fluteboys, then suddenly you're struggling in a hot broiling sun of hell in dust and nettles and poison oak...just like life."

In The Dharma Bums, Kerouac’s Buddhism is joyful, contradictory, and deeply human. The novel is about chosen poverty, friendship, art made without permission, faith without institutions, and the desire to touch something infinite while still wanting wine, laughter, and long conversations. Mountains are climbed. Stillness is practiced. Both matter.

What makes the book feel surprisingly modern is its resistance to certainty. Rather than offering answers, it documents a process: spiritual curiosity without dogma, community without hierarchy, freedom without a fixed destination. In that sense, it feels newly relevant in a contemporary moment marked by burnout, disillusionment, and a renewed hunger for meaning outside traditional structures.

Near the end of the novel, the narrator has clearly made progress toward the enlightenment he seeks:

"Standing on my head before bedtime on that rock roof of the moonlight I could indeed see that the earth was truly upsidedown and man a weird vain beetle full of strange ideas walking around upsidedown and boasting..."

But, in a Socratic spirit, he continues to ponder and ask questions:

"Suppose we suddenly wake up and see that what we thought to be this and that, ain't this and that at all?"
"Are we fallen angels who didn't want to believe that nothing is nothing and so were born to lose our loved ones and dear friends one by one and finally our own life, to see it proved?"

The point isn’t the answers, but the asking.

So, is The Dharma Bums perfect? No. It rambles and resists polish, but that looseness is the point. Like much of Kerouac’s best work, it isn’t interested in refinement so much as sincerity.

I finished it feeling lighter, lonelier, freer, and more awake. A book I’ll return to whenever I forget how to look at the sky. Five stars, no hesitation, and a rare book that leaves you feeling both emptied and full at once.
