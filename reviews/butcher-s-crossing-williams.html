Book Reviews

Butcher's Crossing

John Williams

★★★★★ Read September 21, 2025

Will Andrews walks away from Harvard and civilization and heads into the wilderness. What begins as a young man’s escape from academia soon becomes something far deeper: a search for self. When he joins a buffalo hunt, it isn’t just an adventure — it’s an initiation. From that point on, it’s Andrews vs. himself. Andrews vs. ideals. Andrews vs. the mythology of the West.

On the surface, much of Butcher’s Crossing reads like a “man vs. nature” tale. The buffalo hunt scenes are visceral, relentless, and shocking. Andrews watches the slaughter unfold, and it tears apart his belief in what he thought nature was, or what he needed it to be. At one point Williams writes:

“It came to him that he had sickened and turned away because of his shock at seeing the buffalo, a few moments before proud and noble and full of the dignity of life, now stark and helpless, a length of inert meat, divested of itself… In that murder he had felt the destruction of something within him, and he had not been able to face it.”

But Butcher’s Crossing isn’t all raw brutality. There is much beauty in Andrews' quiet reflections, in the recognition of nature not as a backdrop but as a character that is impartial, magnificent, and utterly indifferent. Williams renders the landscape in such exacting detail that it dwarfs the men both physically and spiritually.

The novel carries immense moral and existential weight. Andrews isn’t just grappling with cold and hunger, but with disillusionment. Williams dismantles the romanticized notions of the “rugged West.” Andrews seeks transcendence and revelation; what he finds is more ambiguous, more broken, but also more honest.

The pacing can test a reader’s patience with its long stretches of wilderness, snow, and silence. But that is its design: the grind, the waiting, the monotony, all pressing in with the weight of reality.

Readers should know: Butcher’s Crossing is not an easy or uplifting novel. Those expecting redemption, heroism, or triumph will leave unsettled. What Williams offers instead is a haunting meditation on illusion and reality, on how ideals shatter when confronted with the indifferent world.

Over time, the novel works its way under your skin. If you value books that unsettle as much as they enlighten, that strip away myth in search of something truer, Butcher’s Crossing is one to devour. It delivers deeply in atmosphere, in moral weight, and character, but it demands just as much from its reader. It’s a journey not only into wilderness, but into the self.
