Book Reviews

Atlas of Unknowable Things

McCormick Templeman

★★★☆☆ Read November 19, 2025

Thank you to St. Martin's Press for providing this book for review consideration via NetGalley. All opinions are my own.

Atlas of Unknowable Things sets out with an irresistible premise: a modern gothic mystery set high in the Rocky Mountains at secluded Hildegard College. Historian Robin Quain arrives for a prestigious residency, seemingly to study ancient manuscripts, but privately determined to uncover a mysterious artifact.

From the outset, the atmospheric strangeness hooked me: strange sounds over the lake, flickering lights between the pines, and faculty members who feel just a shade too polished to be real. Without spoiling anything, it was interesting to follow Robin's shift in perspective as curiosity overtook fear and she felt she might finally be at the center of something meaningful.

Templeman excels in the novel’s imaginative blend of historical detail, botanical lore, and occult intrigue. The setting is lush, inventive, and often the strongest part of the experience.

Where the book faltered for me was in its character work and dialogue. Several lines undercut the carefully crafted atmosphere. A moment like, “he tried to give me handsome-boy sad eyes, but I wasn’t falling for it. Looks only counted for so much” pulled me straight out of the tension. For a story steeped in gothic unease, many character interactions felt too forced or self-conscious to sustain the mood.

The plot hinges on Robin uncovering Hildegard’s secrets, and while the early breadcrumbs were intriguing, her later limitations didn’t feel organic or make a lot of narrative sense.

My frustration with her lack of progress peaked when Robin finally voices the exact question I’d been asking: why is she still here? She recognizes that the situation has tipped from intriguing into outright disorienting and even considers leaving. Up to that moment, the book had been building toward the importance of her discoveries, only to undercut itself with an abrupt shift that made the momentum feel squandered. It left me with the sense that the plot was pulling back the curtain simply to reveal a shrug.

By the end, Robin breaks the fourth wall and addresses the reader directly. I won’t give away what she says, but it’s an interesting thematic gesture that points toward the slipperiness of truth, perception, and the stories we tell ourselves. In practice, though, it didn’t fully smooth over the unevenness I felt in the novel’s middle.

For me, this was an engaging premise with flashes of brilliance, but the inconsistent character dynamics and narrative choices kept it from fully landing. I rate it 2.75 stars, rounded up to 3. That said, I would still recommend it to readers who love enigmatic academic settings, metafictional twists, and stories where atmosphere takes precedence over clarity. Fans of modern gothic mysteries may be more satisfied than I was with the novel’s ambition, experimental structure, and eerie campus vibes.
